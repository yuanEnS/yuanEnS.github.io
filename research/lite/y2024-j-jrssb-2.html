Andrej Srakar’s contribution to the Discussion of ‘Safe testing’ by Grünwald, de Heide, and Koolen

Andrej Srakar

摘要

Paper presented by Grünwald, de Heide, and Koolen presents an extension of e-values to the testing of composite hypotheses in a context they label ‘safe testing’. It discusses what should be a merging of the Fisherian, Neymanian, and Jeffreys-Bayesian interpretations to the testing of hypotheses. Proposed tests based on e-values are safe, i.e. they preserve type-I error guarantees, under proposed optional continuation where the decision to perform a new study may depend on previous outcomes. I provide short remarks related to probability aspects and possibilities in the proposed approach. Namely, construction of the approach is based on supermartingales and relation to previously studied e-processes (Ramdas et al., 2021). Brownian motion is mentioned at the end of the article appendix but not contextualized in more detail. What I question (this is also based on the illustrative Figure 1) is about possible additional extensions in a probability context. For example, when one would want to address the context of composite hypotheses in their dependencies, approaches and stochastic models based on interacting particle systems (such as simple exclusion or simple inclusion processes, or many extensions therein) could be interesting and useful to include. Literature in this area has previously studied relationship to martingales (e.g. Delgadino et al., 2021; Oelschläger, 1984). Other probability theory contexts could also be addressed for future research on e-values. *(abstract via OpenAlex)*

Marco Cattaneo's contribution to the Discussion of “Safe testing” by Grünwald, de Heide, and Koolen

Marco E G V Cattaneo

摘要

This is an excellent paper, neatly summarizing previous results about e-variables, as well as presenting important advances. The possibility of obtaining conservative p-values and confidence intervals even under optional continuation is a substantial advantage of e-values over alternative measures of statistical evidence. Criticisms of the use of p-values often include the advice that p-values should only be interpreted jointly with an estimate of the effect size of interest. Under optional continuation, effect size estimation may be difficult. With preplanned sequential testing, standard estimators (such as maximum likelihood estimators) can be severely biased, but it may be possible to estimate their bias and improve the effect size estimates. In the optional continuation scenario, even the concept of bias seems problematic, since the sequential analysis is not preplanned and unconditional expectations cannot be easily evaluated. Given an effect size of interest δ and an e-variable Eδ0 for each null hypothesis δ=δ0⁠, the convex hull of {δ:Eδ≤1/α} is a conservative (1−α) confidence interval for δ. In analogy with likelihood ratios, it seems quite natural to consider argminδEδ as an estimate of the effect size δ. Have the properties of such ‘minimum e-value’ estimators been studied, in particular under optional continuation? The end of Section 6 discusses the sample size requirements in situations in which stopping is possible after each data point. It is shown that in order to achieve the desired power, hypothesis test based on e-variables may need on average less data than standard tests with fixed sample size. It would be interesting to include also standard sequential tests in the comparison, since they would also be possible in such situations and may also need on average less data than tests with fixed sample size. *(abstract via OpenAlex)*

Samuel Pawel and Leonhard Held’s contribution to the Discussion of ‘Safe Testing’ by Grünwald, de Heide, and Koolen

Samuel Pawel, Leonhard Held

摘要

We congratulate the authors on their paper introducing a ‘safe’ inference framework. However, despite their supposed safety, we have reservations about whether ‘safe’ tests can make the scientific enterprise more reliable. For instance, researchers may still prefer classical non-sequential over sequential study designs, as the latter can be difficult to implement in practice. For example, interim analyses in randomized clinical trials will require unblinding and may as such threaten the integrity of the trial (Ellenberg et al., 2019, Chapter 5). In addition, it has been argued that practical problems in the use of traditional p-values (rather than e-values) stem more from cognitive factors than from the choice of inferential statistics (Gelman, 2016). For example, an excessive focus on testing parameter values of ‘no effect’ while ignoring other relevant parameter values (‘nullism’), unnecessary dichotomization of quantitative information (‘dichotomania’), or treating statistical models as known physical laws rather than speculative assumptions (‘statistical reification’) can all lead to distorted interpretations of study results (Greenland, 2017). Especially with respect to the last point, we believe that naming a statistical test as ‘safe’ can mislead researchers into thinking that the method is ‘always valid’, when this is only true in a certain technical sense and under certain speculative assumptions (e.g. that the underlying data model is correctly specified). For instance, in meta-analysis, a promising application according to the authors, inferences based on e-values will not be ‘safe’ unless the analysis accounts for possible bias, for example, publication bias (Cooper et al., 2019, Chapter 18). In this case, can e-values be adjusted for potential model misspecification (Copas & Eguchi, 2005)? The evidence interpretation of e-values as Bayes factors is more aligned with our view, since Bayes factors quantify relative predictive performance of competing hypotheses without claims about ‘safe’ error rates, yet challenges with nullistic and dichotomous thinking remain. If conventional p-values and confidence intervals were replaced by e-value counterparts, how should results be reported to address these challenges? For testing of one- and two-dimensional parameters, we propose a graphical display inspired by p-value functions and confidence curves (Birnbaum, 1961; Cox, 1958), and their Bayes factor analogues (Pawel et al., 2024). The idea is to plot the e-value as a function of the tested parameter value, see Figure 1 for an illustration. Like the p-value function, this shifts emphasis away from nullistic-dichotomous to gradual-quantitative inferences. Example of an e-value function, i.e. the e-value displayed as a function of the tested parameter value. The graph provides a wealth of information as e-values and conservative p-values (for any tested parameter value), conservative confidence intervals (at any level of interest), and point estimates (the parameter values with the least evidence against them) can be easily read off. In summary, while ‘safe’ tests offer potential advantages, they are not always safe to use and can be affected by the same cognitive issues as other methods. Careful application and contextual interpretation are paramount to their success. *(abstract via OpenAlex)*

While some Bayesian schools refuse to consider the concept of testing the veracity of a null hypothesis, most do propose approaches and methodologies to address this challenge. These mainly rely on Jeffreys’ formalization via two prior distributions (Jeffreys, 1939; Wrinch & Jeffreys, 1919) via what would later come to be the Bayes Factor (Ly et al., 2016; Robert et al., 2009; Wagenmakers & Ly, 2022). In his Theory of Probability, Jeffreys constructs the equivalent of his point estimation prior for tests, although it had little impact on the field until rediscovered by Bayarri and Garcia-Donato (2007). The link of the theory of e-values with right Haar priors reminds us of Pitman’s derivation of the best equivariant estimator (Robert, 2001), even though the fact that Haar priors are improper for the most part proves a major hindrance in a testing situation (DeGroot, 1973). The optional continuation in (a) is an even stronger argument—if from a Bayesian viewpoint—since it is been used to defend the Bayesian paradigm. On the other hand, if sequentially testing e-values, only the first e-value will be interpretable as a coherent Bayes factor. That is, in sequential testing, the product of the e-values does not share the same properties as a product of Bayes factors (i.e. cancellation of terms as a telescoping product). Central to our interest, point (d) above, despite the cautionary added label of an ‘Almost Bayesian Case’, brings a formal way to define least favourable priors (Berger, 1985) in a testing environment, albeit in no clear connection with the reference priors of Bayarri and Garcia-Donato (2007). This derivation, formalized as Theorem 1, provides a reversal of the typical prior choice in testing. A more common stance is to hold the (subjectively chosen) prior distribution on H0 as a starting point—this is an obviously known entity. One may wonder if a dual perspective, i.e. when starting from the prior distribution on H0⁠, would prove fruitful, e.g. in leading to an optimal prior on H1⁠. The paper seems to conclude at a perfect duality with the minimax–maximin result. This also relates to the projection priors we defined in Goutis and Robert (1998) and Dupuis and Robert (2003). As a plus, however, the approach found therein allows for improper priors on the nuisance parameters and thus somehow brings a stronger justification than Berger et al. (1998) in favour of the permanence of an identical measure (on the nuisance parameters) across hypotheses and models. In conclusion, we congratulate the authors on this endeavour but it remains unclear to us (as Bayesians) (i) how to construct the least favourable prior on H0 on a general basis, especially from a computational viewpoint, and, more importantly and (ii) whether it is at all of inferential interest (i.e. whether it degenerates into a point mass). With respect to the sequential directions of the article, we also wonder at the potential connections with sequential Monte Carlo, for instance, towards conducting sequential model choice by constructing efficiently an amalgamated evidence value when the product of Bayes factors is not a Bayes factor (see e.g. Buchholz et al., 2023). 摘要

The potential of e-values (or e-variables) to unite different schools of statistics and allowing optional stopping/continuation in a ‘safe’ manner is a major accomplishment of the authors. The theory implies a change in focus from classical error rates to the expected evidence against the null under the respective hypotheses. Under H0⁠, it is required that the expectation of the evidence against the null, E, should not exceed 1, i.e. EH0[E]≤1⁠, as in formula (1) of ‘Safe testing’. Clearly, using the reciprocal of p-values as a measure of evidence against the null severely violates this criterion as EH0[1/p]=∞⁠. Thus, we see (via a new route) that the routine use of p-values in scientific practice seriously harms the trustworthiness of research findings. Under H1⁠, Bayes factors using a right Haar prior on the scale behave as e-values with an optimal growth rate of the evidence. As stated by the authors, more (nontrivial) e-values exist. As an alternative, I consider fractional Bayes factors (FBFs; O’Hagan, 1995) which are easy to compute and easy to apply as no proper priors need to be formulated. In fractional Bayes methodology, a (minimal) fraction of the data, b, is used to update an improper prior to obtain a proper ‘data-adapted prior’ while the remaining (maximal) fraction is used for computing the evidence (Gilks, 1995; Mulder, 2014). For a simple t-test, the FBF of H1 against H0 is also scale-invariant and given by Γ(nb2)Γ(n−12)Γ(nb−12)Γ(n2)(1+(n−1)−1t2)−n(b−1)/2⁠, where t is the regular t-statistic. The figure illustrates that FBFs (i) are ‘safe’ as requirement (1) under H0 is satisfied, and (ii) behave competitively to a Bayes factor with optimal growth rate under H1 when using a minimal fraction, with even higher growth rates for large (fixed) nonzero effects. Christian Hennig’s contribution to the Discussion of ‘Safe testing’ by Grünwald, de Heide, and Koolen

Christian Hennig

摘要

I appreciate the elegance of the theory regarding (RE)GRO(W) e-variables with optional continuation for combining evidence. Here are some rather problematic aspects. (1) The authors state that ‘there seems to be growing consensus that, in the context of hypothesis testing, p-values should either not be used at all, or at least, with utter care’. I see little consensus that p-values should not be used at all. The request for ‘utter care’ is sensible, but I do not believe that the use of any statistical inference without ‘utter care’ should be advertised. This is exactly what the authors are doing, calling their paper ‘Safe testing’. This is marketing language. Users may trust the supposed ‘safety’ of the approach and may not understand that care is still needed. Actually the term ‘safety’ refers to a specific feature of the proposed approach that is indeed desirable, but many other issues with statistical inference apply to the presented approach just as much (Hennig, 2022). (2) Model assumptions are an issue with p-values and parametric inference in general. In many standard situations, outliers can cause trouble.Considering a standard t-statistic, an extreme outlier will affect the standard error in a stronger way than the mean. The resulting t-statistic will have a low absolute value, potentially leading to p≈1⁠. Regarding traditional approaches to combine evidence, both Fisher’s method (considering −2∑i=1klnpi⁠, where p1,…,pk are p-values from k different studies) and meta-analysis based on standard errors will give a single study with p≈1 and large standard error little influence on the overall result. The situation with e-values is different. One e-value ≈0 can bring down the combining product of e-values. I suspect that outliers can be more problematic for combining e-values than for combining p-values. (3) Although the authors state that e-values can be interpreted in an attractive manner, I am not convinced that their interpretation is clearer than the interpretation of p-values. Probabilities (and consequently p-values) have been given monetary interpretations as well in the literature. When interpreting how large a payout should be seen as large enough for rejection, the authors recur to rejection probabilities, even though the relation between e-values and rejection probabilities is only an inequality, and despite criticizing the choices of rejection thresholds in standard testing as ‘arbitrary’. (4) The GRO-criterion, namely optimizing E(logE)⁠, may not look so attractive to a gambler who is interested in a payout of E rather than logE in a real gamble. *(abstract via OpenAlex)*

I congratulate the authors on this extremely interesting work, having the indisputable merit to introduce formal optimality criteria for testing (GRO, GROW, REGROW) using e-variables, under increasingly complex conditions. An illuminating description of how they relate to each other is offered at the beginning of Section 4. Notably, if the alternative Θ1={θ*} is simple and the true data generating distribution is Pθ*⁠, the GRO(θ*) can be linked (under regularity assumptions) to a notion of oracle prior distributionW0*(⋅)≡W0*(θ*;⋅) on the null set Θ0⁠, yielding via PW0*=∫Pθ(⋅)W0*(dθ) the reverse information projection (Csiszár & Matus, 2003; Grünwald & Mehta, 2020) of Pθ* on the class of Bayesian marginal distributions PW⁠, which stem from all priors W on the null set (see also Koolen & Grünwald, 2022, Theorem 12). With W0*(θ*;⋅) at hand, an oracle would construct the optimal e-variable as E*=pθ*(Y)/pW0*(Y)⁠. In practice, θ* is unknown and so is W0*(θ*;⋅)⁠. One may thus only target e-variables whose expected growth is close to the optimum no matter what θ* actually is. The GROW and REGROW criteria are introduced to this aim. A somewhat natural question is whether the oracle prior can be sensibly inferred from the data, producing where W^0*≡W0*(θ^;⋅) and θ^ is a statistic that conveys information on θ*⁠, in such a way that E^* is an approximately optimal e-variable. Similarly, whenever the statistical model includes a main parameter δ and a nuisance γ, and if the GROW e-variable to test δ is known to be E*=g(Y;γ) in the idealistic case where γ is given, one wonders whether in practice a “good” e-variable E^*=g(Y;γ^) can be obtained by simply plugging a suitable estimator γ^ of γ in g(Y;⋅)⁠. These solutions, which could be regarded as shortcuts to the use of the more rigorous GROW and REGROW criteria, would be reminiscent of empirical Bayes practices in Bayesian inference (see, e.g. Petrone et al., 2014, Sections 2 and 4.2). Intuition can be gathered from the prototypical case of an i.i.d. Gaussian sample Y=(Y1,…,Yn) from N(μ,σ2) , where Θ0={(0,σ):σ>0} is tested versus Θ1={(μ,σ):μ≠0,σ>0}⁠. 摘要

The main result of this paper (Theorem 1) is elegant and satisfying, but in this comment I will concentrate on one of its limitations, namely the condition infW0D(Q‖PW0)<∞⁠. (Analogous conditions are present in all generalizations given in the paper.) Even in the toy binary case with an infinite horizon, this condition is typically violated. Consider, e.g., the case of Markov alternative hypotheses Q considered in Ramdas et al. (2022): a nonIID Markov probability measure (or a Jeffreys-type mixture of such measures) and any exchangeable probability measure are mutually singular. A possible counter-argument is that infinities are irrelevant in real life, and we can always truncate a potentially infinite sequence of observations, but the problem is that our results will depend on the details of truncation. If we do not know the number of observations in advance and just would like to have an online measure of evidence found against the null hypothesis (such as a test martingale), we cannot use Theorem 1. It is interesting that narrow parametric models with a given simple alternative (as in Ramdas et al., 2022, or Vovk et al., 2022, Sect. 9.2) admit a simple solution: just replace Grünwald et al.’s average in the denominator of E* (see Theorem 1) by the supremum. But this idea does not work for nontrivial nonparametric models (Vovk et al., 2022, Remark 9.8). Our book (Vovk et al., 2022, Part III) presents a framework, which we call conformal testing, for testing nonparametric null hypotheses (first of all the hypothesis of exchangeability) in general situations typical of machine learning. Restricting ourselves to the class of conformal test martingales with a given conformity measure, we construct an optimal testing procedure, the Bayes–Kelly algorithm, whose optimality property is akin to Grünwald et al.’s one; it is stated explicitly in the extended version of this comment (Vovk, 2024, Theorem 3.2). We spell out the details of the procedure and prove general optimality properties (not restricted to conformal testing) in the binary case (Vovk et al., 2022, Sect. 9.2), but the procedure itself is general, and the efficiency of conformal testing is demonstrated in empirical studies reported in (Vovk et al., 2022, Chap. 8). Of course, it is not surprising that Theorem 1 has limitations (and the authors discuss some), but it is a great first step that opens up interesting directions of further research. One such direction is to design optimal (in Grünwald et al.’s sense) conformity measures in conformal testing. Ruodu Wang

摘要

The contribution of Peter Grünwald, Rianne de Heide, and Wouter Koolen (GDK henceforth) is an important piece in the theory of e-values and e-processes. Methods based on e-values are developed to overcome several drawbacks (but not all, of course) in traditional methods based on p-values and those involving statistical malpractice. Due to GDK and a few others around the same time (around 2019) the research theme of e-values has been growing rapidly and healthily over the past few years in multiple exciting directions. An overview was provided by Ramdas et al. (2023), but there has been much more progresses since then. In addition to the various statistical advantages discussed by GDK, there is a notable advantage from a mathematical point of view: The theory of martingales is essential for this methodology, allowing statisticians to appreciate and contribute to this classic theory in probability. Conversely, the theory of e-values has successfully attracted probabilists and financial mathematicians to join the journey of statistics. Methods based on e-values have Type I error guarantee under finite sample and optional stopping, as well illustrated in GDK and the recent literature. These methods are sometimes criticized for their power. Traditionally, power under a specific alternative probability Q is measured by Q(rejecting the null)⁠. For a fixed-sample testing problem, with this definition of power and a prespecified Type I error control, methods based on e-values typically have less power compared to those based on properly chosen p-values (a result of the Neymann–Pearson lemma). Nevertheless, power in the context of e-test may be defined differently: For a specific alternative Q, the growth-rate optimality in GDK is a criterion to maximize ΠQ(E):=EQ[logE] over e-variables E; see Theorem 1 of GDK. Section 2 of Shafer (2021) also has an extensive discussion on this quantity, with an intimate connection to Kelly’s criterion in investment theory. The desirability of optimizing ΠQ(E) and its composite-alternative version is crucial for the main results of GDK. The quantity ΠQ(E) is called the e-power of E under Q by Vovk and Wang (2024), where it is used to define relative efficiency of e-tests. Many papers on e-tests have adapted this criterion as a notion of power. Despite the intuitive reasons to use ΠQ(E) to represent power, there are several issues to discuss. A main argument in GDK and related papers for using ΠQ(E) is that, in a sequential setting, e-variables E1,E2,… are meant to be multiplicative. For simplicity, suppose that ΠQ(Et) is the same for each t∈N⁠. Under some conditions and in an asymptotic sense, ΠQ(E1) is the average rate of growth as t→∞ for the e-processes M=(Mt)t∈N given by However, in practice, e-tests are used mainly in finite sample and often not multiplicative; for instance, Waudby-Smith and Ramdas (2024) use e-processes built from a betting strategy or a weighted arithmetic average. In such cases, the interpretation of the e-power of individual e-variables is not clear. Even under multiplication and independence, we can find simple, perhaps pathological, examples to scrutinize the intuition of the e-power measured by ΠQ⁠. Below we use P for a simple null. Suppose that E1,E2,… are independent e-variables for P. If E1 satisfies ΠQ(E1)=−∞ like in (a) above and Et has finite e-power for all t≥2⁠, then the process M built in (1) satisfies ΠQ(Mt)=−∞ for all t∈N⁠, no matter how fast it grows after t=1 (in particular, one can easily build M with Mt→∞ in probability under Q). In each example above, we find e-variables or e-processes that are intuitively powerful but have negative infinite e-power. Taking a reciprocal in (b) and (c), we also find e-processes with positive infinite e-power but declines to 0 with probability 1 under Q. On the positive side, Proposition 1 of Vovk and Wang (2024) showed that ΠQ enjoys a natural and desirable property: Combining several independent e-tests of positive power (measured by ΠQ⁠) by partial products, averages, or U-statistics always results in positive power. The purpose of the discussion here is to explain that e-power deserves more discussions to fully understand its advantages and limitations. Neil Dey, Ryan Martin, Jonathan P Williams

摘要

Congratulations to Grünwald, de Heide, and Koolen (GHK) for their contribution to the exciting and rapidly growing literature on safe/anytime-valid inference.
