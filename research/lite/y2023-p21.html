SiM Survival Analysis · 发表于 2023-03-27

Adjusting for informative cluster size in pseudo‐value‐based regression approaches with clustered time to event data

Samuel Anyaso‐Samuel, Somnath Datta

摘要

Informative cluster size (ICS) arises in situations with clustered data where a latent relationship exists between the number of participants in a cluster and the outcome measures. Although this phenomenon has been sporadically reported in the statistical literature for nearly two decades now, further exploration is needed in certain statistical methodologies to avoid potentially misleading inferences. For inference about population quantities without covariates, inverse cluster size reweightings are often employed to adjust for ICS. Further, to study the effect of covariates on disease progression described by a multistate model, the pseudo‐value regression technique has gained popularity in time‐to‐event data analysis. We seek to answer the question: “How to apply pseudo‐value regression to clustered time‐to‐event data when cluster size is informative?” ICS adjustment by the reweighting method can be performed in two steps; estimation of marginal functions of the multistate model and fitting the estimating equations based on pseudo‐value responses, leading to four possible strategies. We present theoretical arguments and thorough simulation experiments to ascertain the correct strategy for adjusting for ICS. A further extension of our methodology is implemented to include informativeness induced by the intracluster group size. We demonstrate the methods in two real‐world applications: (i) to determine predictors of tooth survival in a periodontal study and (ii) to identify indicators of ambulatory recovery in spinal cord injury patients who participated in locomotor‐training rehabilitation.

SiM High-Dimensional Survival Analysis · 发表于 2023-03-25

Penalized maximum likelihood inference under the mixture cure model in sparse data

Changchang Xu, Shelley B. Bull

摘要

Introduction: When a study sample includes a large proportion of long‐term survivors, mixture cure (MC) models that separately assess biomarker associations with long‐term recurrence‐free survival and time to disease recurrence are preferred to proportional‐hazards models. However, in samples with few recurrences, standard maximum likelihood can be biased. Objective and Methods: We extend Firth‐type penalized likelihood (FT‐PL) developed for bias reduction in the exponential family to the Weibull‐logistic MC, using the Jeffreys invariant prior. Via simulation studies based on a motivating cohort study, we compare parameter estimates of the FT‐PL method to those by ML, as well as type 1 error (T1E) and power obtained using likelihood ratio statistics. Results: In samples with relatively few events, the Firth‐type penalized likelihood estimates (FT‐PLEs) have mean bias closer to zero and smaller mean squared error than maximum likelihood estimates (MLEs), and can be obtained in samples where the MLEs are infinite. Under similar T1E rates, FT‐PL consistently exhibits higher statistical power than ML in samples with few events. In addition, we compare FT‐PL estimation with two other penalization methods (a log‐F prior method and a modified Firth‐type method) based on the same simulations. Discussion: Consistent with findings for logistic and Cox regressions, FT‐PL under MC regression yields finite estimates under stringent conditions, and better bias‐and‐variance balance than the other two penalizations. The practicality and strength of FT‐PL for MC analysis is illustrated in a cohort study of breast cancer prognosis with long‐term follow‐up for recurrence‐free survival.

Biometrics Spatial & Time Series Latent Variables & Mixtures · 发表于 2023-03-25

Detecting the Spatial Clustering of Exposure–Response Relationships with Estimation Error: A Novel Spatial Scan Statistic

Wei Wang, Sheng Li, Tao Zhang, Fei Yin, Yue Ma

摘要

Detecting the spatial clustering of the exposure–response relationship (ERR) between environmental risk factors and health-related outcomes plays important roles in disease control and prevention, such as identifying highly sensitive regions, exploring the causes of heterogeneous ERRs, and designing region-specific health intervention measures. However, few studies have focused on this issue. A possible reason is that the commonly used cluster-detecting tool, spatial scan statistics, cannot be used for multivariate spatial datasets with estimation error, such as the ERR, which is often defined by a vector with its covariance estimated by a regression model. Such spatial datasets have been produced in abundance in the last decade, which suggests the importance of developing a novel cluster-detecting tool applicable for multivariate datasets with estimation error. In this work, by extending the classic scan statistic, we developed a novel spatial scan statistic called the estimation-error-based scan statistic (EESS), which is applicable for both univariate and multivariate datasets with estimation error. Then, a two-stage analytic process was proposed to detect the spatial clustering of ERRs in practical studies. A published motivating example and a simulation study were used to validate the performance of EESS. The results show that the clusters detected by EESS can efficiently reflect the clustering heterogeneity and yield more accurate ERR estimates by adjusting for such heterogeneity.

Biometrika Testing & Uncertainty · 发表于 2023-03-24

Hybrid confidence intervals for informative uniform asymptotic inference after model selection

A McCloskey

摘要

I propose a new type of confidence interval for correct asymptotic inference after using data to select a model of interest without assuming any model is correctly specified. This hybrid confidence interval is constructed by combining techniques from the selective inference and post-selection inference literatures to yield a short confidence interval across a wide range of data realizations. I show that hybrid confidence intervals have correct asymptotic coverage, uniformly over a large class of probability distributions that do not bound scaled model parameters. I illustrate the use of these confidence intervals in the problem of inference after using the lasso objective function to select a regression model of interest and provide evidence of their desirable length and coverage properties in small samples via a set of Monte Carlo experiments that entail a variety of different data distributions as well as an empirical application to the predictors of diabetes disease progression.

Biometrics Meta-Analysis & Data Integration · 发表于 2023-03-24

An Efficient Data Integration Scheme for Synthesizing Information from Multiple Secondary Datasets for the Parameter Inference of the Main Analysis

Chixiang Chen, Ming Wang, Shuo Chen

摘要

Many observational studies and clinical trials collect various secondary outcomes that may be highly correlated with the primary endpoint. These secondary outcomes are often analyzed in secondary analyses separately from the main data analysis. However, these secondary outcomes can be used to improve the estimation precision in the main analysis. We propose a method called multiple information borrowing (MinBo) that borrows information from secondary data (containing secondary outcomes and covariates) to improve the efficiency of the main analysis. The proposed method is robust against model misspecification of the secondary data. Both theoretical and case studies demonstrate that MinBo outperforms existing methods in terms of efficiency gain. We apply MinBo to data from the Atherosclerosis Risk in Communities study to assess risk factors for hypertension.

Biometrics Latent Variables & Mixtures · 发表于 2023-03-23

Supervised Convex Clustering

Minjie Wang, Tianyi Yao, Genevera I. Allen

摘要

Clustering has long been a popular unsupervised learning approach to identify groups of similar objects and discover patterns from unlabeled data in many applications. Yet, coming up with meaningful interpretations of the estimated clusters has often been challenging precisely due to their unsupervised nature. Meanwhile, in many real-world scenarios, there are some noisy supervising auxiliary variables, for instance, subjective diagnostic opinions, that are related to the observed heterogeneity of the unlabeled data. By leveraging information from both supervising auxiliary variables and unlabeled data, we seek to uncover more scientifically interpretable group structures that may be hidden by completely unsupervised analyses. In this work, we propose and develop a new statistical pattern discovery method named supervised convex clustering (SCC) that borrows strength from both information sources and guides towards finding more interpretable patterns via a joint convex fusion penalty. We develop several extensions of SCC to integrate different types of supervising auxiliary variables, to adjust for additional covariates, and to find biclusters. We demonstrate the practical advantages of SCC through simulations and a case study on Alzheimer's disease genomics. Specifically, we discover new candidate genes as well as new subtypes of Alzheimer's disease that can potentially lead to better understanding of the underlying genetic mechanisms responsible for the observed heterogeneity of cognitive decline in older adults.

Identifying regions of interest in mammogram images

Shu Jiang, Jiguo Cao, Graham A. Colditz

摘要

Screening mammography is the primary preventive strategy for early detection of breast cancer and an essential input to breast cancer risk prediction and application of prevention/risk management guidelines. Identifying regions of interest within mammogram images that are associated with 5- or 10-year breast cancer risk is therefore clinically meaningful. The problem is complicated by the irregular boundary issue posed by the semi-circular domain of the breast area within mammograms. Accommodating the irregular domain is especially crucial when identifying regions of interest, as the true signal comes only from the semi-circular domain of the breast region, and noise elsewhere. We address these challenges by introducing a proportional hazards model with imaging predictors characterized by bivariate splines over triangulation. The model sparsity is enforced with the group lasso penalty function. We apply the proposed method to the motivating Joanne Knight Breast Health Cohort to illustrate important risk patterns and show that the proposed method is able to achieve higher discriminatory performance.

Disease progression based feature screening for ultrahigh‐dimensional survival‐associated biomarkers

Mengjiao Peng, Liming Xiang

摘要

Summary: The increased availability of ultrahigh‐dimensional biomarker data and the high demand of identifying biomarkers importantly related to survival outcomes made feature screening methods commonplace in the analysis of cancer genome data. When survival outcomes include endpoints of overall survival (OS) and time‐to‐progression (TTP), a high concordance is typically found in both endpoints in cancer studies, namely, patients' OS would most likely be extended when tumour progression is delayed. Existing screening procedures are often performed on a single survival endpoint only and may result in biased selection of features for OS in ignorance of disease progression. We propose a novel feature screening method by incorporating information of TTP into the selection of important biomarker predictors for more accurate inference of OS subsequent to disease progression. The proposal is based on the rank of correlation between individual features and the conditional distribution of OS given observations of TTP. It is advantageous for its flexible model nature, which requires no marginal model assumption for each endpoint, and its minimal computational cost for implementation. Theoretical results show its ranking consistency, sure screening and false rate control properties. Simulation results demonstrate that the proposed screener leads to more accurate feature selection than the method without considering the prior observations of disease progression. An application to breast cancer genome data illustrates its practical utility and facilitates disease classification using selected biomarker predictors.

JASA Networks & Graphs Latent Variables & Mixtures · 发表于 2023-03-22

Network Inference Using the Hub Model and Variants

Zhibing He, Yunpeng Zhao, Peter Bickel, Charles Weko, Dan Cheng, Jirui Wang

摘要

Statistical network analysis primarily focuses on inferring the parameters of an observed network. In many applications, especially in the social sciences, the observed data is the groups formed by individual subjects. In these applications, the network is itself a parameter of a statistical model. Zhao and Weko propose a model-based approach, called the hub model, to infer implicit networks from grouping behavior. The hub model assumes that each member of the group is brought together by a member of the group called the hub. The set of members which can serve as a hub is called the hub set. The hub model belongs to the family of Bernoulli mixture models. Identifiability of Bernoulli mixture model parameters is a notoriously difficult problem. This article proves identifiability of the hub model parameters and estimation consistency under mild conditions. Furthermore, this article generalizes the hub model by introducing a model component that allows hubless groups in which individual nodes spontaneously appear independent of any other individual. We refer to this additional component as the null component. The new model bridges the gap between the hub model and the degenerate case of the mixture model—the Bernoulli product. Identifiability and consistency are also proved for the new model. In addition, a penalized likelihood approach is proposed to estimate the hub set when it is unknown. Supplementary materials for this article are available online. *(abstract via OpenAlex)*

JRSSB Bayesian Methods · 发表于 2023-03-22

Monotone response surface of multi-factor condition: estimation and Bayes classifiers

Ying Kuen Cheung, Keith M Diaz

摘要

We formulate the estimation of monotone response surface of multiple factors as the inverse of an iteration of partially ordered classifier ensembles. Each ensemble (called product-of-independent-probability-escalation (PIPE)-classifiers) is a projection of Bayes classifiers on the constrained space. We prove that the inverse of PIPE-classifiers (iPIPE) exists, and propose algorithms to efficiently compute iPIPE by reducing the space over which optimisation is conducted. The methods are applied in analysis and simulation settings where the surface dimension is higher than what the isotonic regression literature typically considers. Simulation shows that iPIPE-based credible intervals achieve nominal coverage probability and are more precise compared to unconstrained estimation.

Biometrics Machine Learning Genetics & Omics · 发表于 2023-03-22

Gene expression data analysis: a statistical and machine learning perspective. PankajBarah, DhrubaKumar Bhattacharyya, JugalKumar Kalita (2022). Boca Raton, Florida and London. CRC Press; Taylor and Francis. https://doi.org/10.1201/9780429322655

Amrita Chattopadhyay

摘要

Gene expression profiling and analysis is an important research area that helps fathoming the underlying molecular mechanisms of complex diseases. Current availability of high-throughput technologies, laboratory investigation protocols along with bioinformatics methodologies allow identification of transcripts and quantification of gene function, toward decoding dynamic biological processes played by RNA as the intermediary step between the genome and the proteome. Explicit understanding of the above enables reliable scientific conclusions. RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) technology, which was introduced in 2008 (Jazayeri et al., 2015), has revolutionized exponentially in the past decade and have evolved from being only a gene expression quantification tool to a powerful resource for discovery of new transcripts, characterization of alternative splicing variants or new cell types (via single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq)), and biomarker detection for drug screening (Hong et al., 2020). Applications of RNA-seq is not only restricted to the genomics community, but expands to being utilized for developing toolkits potentially serving varied scientific purposes for the research community (Stark et al., 2019). Over the years, multitude of protocols and analysis pipelines for RNA-seq have been published, making it an intriguing and challenging discipline for new users to delve into. Therefore, it is important to gain perspectives into the fundamental concepts of biology and functional genomics, data types, analysis strategies, existing methodologies: their advantages and caveats, and an explanation of the results that are revealed at the end of each analytic route. This would potentially allow readers a holistic knowledge about the dynamic biological processes at both cellular and organismal level. This book attempts to provide an easy understanding and guidance for users who have no prior experience of gene expression analysis and lacks the concepts needed to comprehend it. The book consists of a total of eight chapters which can be broadly classified into four distinct sections. The first section includes an introductory chapter (Chapter 1) with brief explanations of each of the topics that have been touched, along the way. The second section contains Chapters 2–4 comprising mostly the fundamental concepts and backgrounds required for readers to learn gene-expression analysis. Chapter 2 introduces systems theory, particularly its application in the hierarchical information flow of genetic information within complex biological systems via top-down or bottom-up models, pertaining to different Omics data. It then proceeds to explain in a succinct way, the central dogma, DNA-replication, transcription and translation, and the ambiguities of central dogma, that led to the understanding of reverse transcriptase and complimentary-DNAs (cDNAs). Chapter 3 focuses on gene expression, its history, descriptions on low-throughput (northern blotting, ribonuclease protection assay, qRT-PCR (real-time quantitative reverse transcription-PCR), and SAGE (serial analysis of gene expression)), and high-throughput technologies (microarray, RNA-seq, scRNA-seq); types of RNA-Seq data and gene-expression databases. The authors use small paragraphs to explain each of the topics, summarizing the relevant information. Procedures to access datasets are important information for freshers of this discipline, which could have added value to this chapter, however, this information was missing. Chapter 4 discusses statistical and machine learning foundations as preceding knowledge for gene expression analysis. The statistical concepts are explained briefly with equations and comprises topics such as probability theory, hypothesis testing methods, exact tests, common data distributions, false discovery rates and, maximal likelihood estimation. Although examples for some of the abovementioned topics have been provided, better illustrations of applications that could have provided more clarity for the readers are lacking. The next part includes machine learning topic that spans almost 73 pages and is quite comprehensive and well explained, providing readers with a glimpse into variety of models, training–testing–validation and evaluation procedures, learning methods, similarity and dissimilarity measures, and partitioning and clustering approaches. Machine learning applications in healthcare services assists in crucial aspects such as clinical decision making, detection of diseases, and personalized medicine toward saving lives. As machine learning is utilized profusely toward identification of biomarkers, it is an important technique that needs to be understood well. The goal is to use machine-learning based equipment and software that can replace or support the existing diagnostic and treatment options for an overall improvement of the healthcare systems while lowering the cost of care. Hence, this is an important and relevant topic and the authors have provided well researched material towards educating the readers and providing them a foundation course toward application into gene-expression data. The third section is composed of Chapters 5 and 6 that discusses methods and techniques for conducting co-expression analysis and differential gene expression analysis, respectively. Co-expression analysis enables identification of genes with similar expression patterns that regulate various biological conditions or phenotypes, based on certain statistical proximity metrics. On the other hand, differential expression analysis enables identification of genes that demonstrate quantitative changes in expression levels between different experimental groups. Both the above methods are important and profusely used in disease-associated transcriptome profiling toward prioritizing regulatory candidate disease genes. Methodologies and approaches to identify co-expression patterns were primarily the focus of Chapter 5. Explanations on types and patterns of gene co-expression, and measures to identify co-expressed genes are provided briefly with clear examples. Descriptions are provided for various aspects and stages of co-expression analysis pipelines such as preprocessing, clustering, query-specific cluster ranking and selection, cluster-based analysis, and validation for different data types (microarray, and sequencing data). In-depth discussions are then provided for each of the above-mentioned analysis approaches with respect to the data type to be processed; such as microarray data, RNAseq data, or scRNAseq data. This was impressively done and can be used as a reference while conducting data analysis on real genomic data. Chapter 6, similarly, gives descriptions of analysis pipelines comprising of detailed preprocessing procedures followed by methodologies to conduct differential gene expression (DE) analysis. A list of statistical tests, both parametric and non-parametric along with numerical descriptions and simplified examples are provided. Validation procedures and downstream network analysis are also described. R-packages and other tools and repositories that allow both co-expression pattern and DE analysis are also described in a succinct way. The authors have succeeded to cover all necessary information, competently through simple and easy to understand descriptions which make the information appropriate for readers without any prior experience in this field. The final section includes Chapters 7 and 8. Chapter 7 focuses on tools and repositories related to the topic, that are popularly used by bioinformaticians and computational biologists. Microarray, RNA-seq, scRNAseq techniques lead to clinically relevant data and large repositories, that houses multitude of samples. The unprecedented rate at which such scientific knowledge is being generated and shared, computational, mathematical and statistical expertise are important toward archiving, retrieving, and analyzing such huge biological data. This chapter provides a comprehensively curated content of tools, databases, and webservers for various steps of the pipeline leading to gene-expression analysis. The contents of this chapter are of extreme relevance and significantly contribute to the value of the book. The chapter is divided into subtopics such as preprocessing tools, discretization techniques, dimensionality reduction and quality control steps, and gene expression analysis tools. Under each topic, the authors provide a detailed description of an extensive list of software and repositories, with tabulated information, and comparative comments that would guide readers to choose the relevant tool based on the suitability and the aim of their study. Furthermore, hyperlinks are provided for convenience of readers. Finally, Chapter 8 summarizes the contents by highlighting the major topics that is handled through this book, notably, co-expression analysis, differential co-expression analysis, and differential expression analysis using statistical and machine-learning approaches. Finally, the authors conclude by discussing about the challenges and issues that are associated with transcriptome research. The target audience of this book is future computational biologists who are venturing into the world of genomics and clinico-genomics studies. The material is relevant and important. The topics are organized well. The writing is smooth and easy to understand even for a non-native English speaker. The topics does not go into too much depth but provides enough information to have an overall understanding. The chapters on fundamentals of machine learning and software and tools, deserve special mention for the carefully collected information and the extensive research that went behind them. The book however suffers from poorly represented and amateurish figures and illustrations. Good illustrations are a way to attract readers especially the inexperienced ones, into exploring the material. In my opinion, the book may fall short in this area and as a result may lose some potential readers. Also, more example problems with example data illustrating each of the contents would have really enhanced the material. Lastly, some typographical error exists and should be rectified in the future editions. Despite few shortcomings, the book is a good reference material in this rapidly evolving and important field of study. I would recommend it as a reference textbook for undergraduate and masters’ students who are pursuing genetics, biostatistics, and bioinformatics. I appreciate that I was given an opportunity to review the book and I praise the authors for their excellent achievement. *(abstract via OpenAlex)*

Biometrics Testing & Uncertainty Clinical Trials Bayesian Methods · 发表于 2023-03-22

Confidence intervals for discrete data in clinical research By VivekPradhan, Ashis K.Gangopadhyay, Sandeep M.Menon, CynthiaBasu, TathagataBanerjee, Chapman and Hall. 2021. pp. 226. $119.95. (hbk). ISBN: 978‐1138048980

Naitee Ting

摘要

In the clinical development of new drugs, research pharmaceutical industry and biotech companies (known as sponsors) design and execute clinical trials in order to evaluate the efficacy and safety of the study drug. Clinical data are observed and collected from trial participants. These data are analyzed and organized to support clinical study reports. Sponsors that develop the new drugs will submit these study reports to U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA) for review. If FDA considers this new drug is efficacious, and, relatively safe, then this new drug would be approved for general patient population. Discrete data are very common in clinical trials. One example is known as “responder analysis.” After the completion of a clinical trial, the physician evaluates each patient and classifies the patient as a “responder” or a “nonresponder.” This is a binary classification of treatment results for each patient. The responder analysis is to study the proportion of responders among all trial participants. If this proportion is high, then it is likely that the study drug can be approved by FDA. In most of clinical trials, two treatment groups (one group of patients receives the test drug treatment, and the other receives control treatment) are compared for efficacy. If it turns out that more proportion of responders is observed from the test drug group than the control group, then the test drug can be approved for general patient population. In this book, Chapters 4 and 5 provide extensive materials to cover treatment comparisons of two independent binomial proportions. These methods can be very useful to practitioners. In some situations, when developing drugs for the treatment of rare diseases, because of difficulty in patient recruitment, “single arm trial” can be found in practice. A single arm trial means the clinical trial treats every participant only with the study medication, without any control group. When this is the case, methods for analysis of one binomial population can be very useful. Another application can be found in crossover clinical trials, or matched case-control epidemiology studies—these research work can be based on the paired binomial data. Chapter 3 of this book covers the one binomial proportion situation, and Chapter 6 focuses on paired binomial proportions. In clinical trial applications, sometimes the interest is in recurring events. For example, in treatment of diabetic patients, hypoglycemia can be a very severe adverse event. In the duration of the study treatment, most patients are not expected to develop hypoglycemia. Some patients could develop one episode. Other patients may develop repeated events. In this application, confidence intervals on count data can be useful. Another example could be chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) exacerbation. In treating COPD patients, the number of exacerbations experienced by patients in a treatment group may serve as one of the primary efficacy endpoints. This is another application of count data. Chapter 7 of this book covers count data and confidence intervals on rate. In development of new drugs, typically many molecules are discovered as drug candidates. Only the best candidates would have the opportunity entering the clinical development stage. Clinical trials are divided into four phases—Phases I, II, III, and IV, in a sequential manner. Every phase of clinical development can be viewed as a process of weeding out unsuccessful drug candidates. In other words, only the very best drug candidate gets the chance to enter the later phase clinical development, and only the ones that can objectively and scientifically demonstrate both drug efficacy and drug safety can be approved by FDA. Hence for every drug candidate at every stage of development process, a “Go/NoGo” decision has to be made about the candidate as to whether it deserves to be progressed into the next step of development. This “Go/NoGo” decision can be thought of as a confirmatory question in drug development. However, every stage of new drug development can also be viewed as a learning process. Development team members are always asking many questions about the drug candidate. All of these questions are exploratory in nature—people are curious, and it is always important to learn the efficacy and safety associated with each drug candidate. Based on this understanding, every clinical question can be divided into a confirmatory question or an exploratory question. The confirmatory question is about the “Go/NoGo” decision, and all other questions are exploratory. In statistical applications, the method to deal with a confirmatory question is hypothesis testing, and the method to deal with an exploratory question is estimation—either point estimation or interval estimation. Another consideration is about the noninferiority decisions. This happens in active-controlled clinical trials. In other words, the Phase III clinical trial is designed to compare a test drug against another drug for the same indication, and this other drug has already been approved by regulatory agencies. Such an approved drug is known as an active control agent. Noninferiority trials are designed with a prespecified margin, which must have been agreed between the sponsor and the regulatory agency. The Phase III comparison is to demonstrate that the lower confidence limit of treatment difference (test drug mean subtract active control mean) should be above the negative value of this margin (when a positive value means better efficacy). Although the comparison is based on the one-sided confidence limit, the decision itself is of a nature as the one-sided hypothesis testing. In other words, the efficacy of the test drug has to be above a predefined quantity, in order for the test drug to be approvable. Again, this approval decision is fundamentally a “Go/NoGo” decision, the only difference is that the noninferiority decision is expressed in terms of a “one-sided confidence interval.” Because the only confirmatory question in drug development is about a “Go/NoGo” decision, most of other clinical questions are exploratory. Estimation is more useful in most of clinical applications. Also, confidence intervals are much more informative than a simple p-value. Chapter 2 of this book covers the p-value discussion from the American Statistical Association (ASA) statement. This is a very valid and relevant point, indicating that estimation, either point estimation or interval estimation, is more useful in applications than statistical hypothesis testing. This book is about confidence intervals. In fact, the emphasis on the confidence interval makes this book very useful for practitioners. There are seven chapters in this book. The first chapter is a brief overview, which covers both the frequentist approach and the Bayesian approach of constructing confidence intervals on discrete data. This chapter also mentions statistical inference, and how statistical thinking established the scientific foundation of clinical trials and modern drug development. Chapter 2 discusses various viewpoints on the “p-value,” specifically, it brings up the ASA statement on the p-value. As mentioned earlier, in clinical trial applications, most of the scientific questions are exploratory in nature. Hence confidence intervals are much more useful than p-values. It is very sensible that this book focuses on confidence interval approaches to handle clinical research questions. In statistical applications to solve real-world problems, confidence interval approaches are much more useful than p-values. The main body of this book includes Chapters 3–7. In these chapters, the authors present a wide range of statistical methods for calculating confidence intervals. For each method, this book introduces the theoretical derivation, the background, and the implementation using either SAS code or R. Many methods covered in this book have existing SAS procedures available. On the other hand, a lot of methods have to be programmed using SAS or R. For most of the applications, the book brings a numerical example (mostly based on clinical trial data) and uses SAS or R to achieve the computation. The results are illustrated to help readers in understanding how each method can be implemented in practice. Furthermore, performance of these methods is also displayed and compared using coverage probabilities obtained from the calculated confidence intervals. Chapter 3 provides methods for calculating confidence intervals on one sample binomial proportion. Broadly speaking, there are three major categories in establishing confidence intervals on one binomial proportion—asymptotic approaches, exact methods, and Bayesian intervals. Within each category, the authors introduce many methods and offer details of each method. Some of these methods are well known and available for decades, and some are relatively new. The authors provide a rather comprehensive list of methods. As mentioned above, in the development of drugs treating rare diseases, because of difficulty in patient recruitment, “single arm trial” can be found in practice. When this is the case, methods introduced in this chapter can be useful to practitioners. Chapter 4 discusses confidence intervals on differences between two independent binomial proportions. Differences between two independent proportions is frequently observed in clinical trials. As mentioned earlier, “responder analysis” is a common practice in many clinical settings. When comparing the drug efficacy of two groups (e.g., test treatment vs. control), development teams often study the differences in the proportion of responders between these two treatments. This chapter focuses on the difference p1– p2 and proposes ways to construct confidence intervals on this difference. In this chapter, the authors introduce many methods to perform the calculation. It starts with asymptotic methods and then moves into exact methods. Also introduced in Chapter 4 of this book is a Bayesian approach to compute credible intervals. Relative risk or risk ratio (RR) is another commonly used metric for comparing two independent binomial proportions in clinical trial applications. Chapter 5 covers confidence interval methods for RR. Similar to Chapter 4, the book starts with asymptotic methods and then moves into exact methods. Furthermore, Chapter 5 also introduces approaches to establish Bayesian credible intervals. Chapter 6 presents methods to calculate confidence intervals for paired binomial proportions. The book mentioned that in crossover trials there may be applications of paired proportions. However, in my personal experience, I have never seen such an application. Most of crossover clinical trials are designed for Phase I bioequivalence studies (or other Phase I trials including bioavailability, food effect, and/or drug–drug-interaction trials). In these clinical trials, the primary variables of interest are pharmacokinetic (PK) parameters. PK parameters are generally believed to follow log-normal distribution, and they tend to be continuous variables. I can imagine that in certain situations, crossover trials are applied to study drug efficacy like in Phase II or III applications and that the primary efficacy endpoint is a binary variable (such as responder status). But those applications are not common in drug development practice. On the other hand, in epidemiology research, case control is an important study type. For case-control studies, first a number of cases (patients with the disease under study, e.g., a certain type of cancer) are identified and recruited into the study. Next, for each case, find one or a few matching controls. A control subject is a person without this disease, but with similar characteristics (e.g., same gender, similar age, occupation, …) as the case. After the cases and controls are identified, data are collected from these participants and, finally, these data are analyzed. Binomial data are very common in case-control studies. Therefore, the application of this book can also include epidemiology studies, or, more generally, the book could be useful for public health applications. In Chapter 6, the authors provide a number of asymptotic approaches including both frequentist and Bayesian methods. It also talks about exact confidence intervals for paired binomial proportions. One more section of Chapter 6 discusses confidence intervals with missing data. Finally, Chapter 7 presents confidence interval methods for rate. This chapter offers a rather comprehensive treatment of count data. Again, count data are an important data type in clinical research. A reference of confidence intervals on rate parameter observed from count data is useful. The chapter starts with Poisson distribution and focuses on the confidence interval of the rate parameter λ. Then the chapter covers confidence intervals for mean and other count data models. Before wrapping up this chapter, the authors cover Bayesian credible intervals. Overall speaking, this book delivered what the authors hoped to achieve—"Confidence Intervals for Discrete Data in Clinical Research." We demonstrate the performance of the proposed methods through simulation studies and apply these methods to estimate the efficiency gain of covariate adjustment in Covid-19 therapeutic trials.

Biometrika Causal Inference · 发表于 2023-03-14

Ancestor regression in linear structural equation models

C Schultheiss, P Bühlmann

摘要

Summary: We present a new method for causal discovery in linear structural equation models. We propose a simple technique based on statistical testing in linear models that can distinguish between ancestors and non-ancestors of any given variable. Naturally, this approach can then be extended to estimating the causal order among all variables. We provide explicit error control for false causal discovery, at least asymptotically. This holds true even under Gaussianity, where other methods fail due to non-identifiable structures. These Type I error guarantees come at the cost of reduced power. Additionally, we provide an asymptotically valid goodness-of-fit p-value for assessing whether multivariate data stem from a linear structural equation model.
